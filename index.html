Research

The Ages of the Nearest Open Clusters

Open clusters have served as long-standing benchmarks for testing how stellar evolution models depend upon stellar mass; they contain stars spanning a broad range of masses (including substellar masses and even planets) at the same age and composition. Despite this, their potential remains unfulfilled because their absolute ages are uncertain by 20% in most cases.

This project shows a novel way in which one can determine nearby open cluster ages (within 350 pc) using red giants and optical long-baseline interferometry with the CHARA Array. Interferometric measurements can directly constrain the angular diameters of red giants, which, when combined with distances and bolometric fluxes, allow fundamental stellar properties such as radius, luminosity, and effective temperature to be determined. These observational constraints can then be compared with stellar evolutionary models to determine the age of the star and thus constrain the age of the cluster itself.

As a preliminary sample, six benchmark clusters have been chosen for this study. These include: Ruprecht 147 (Patel et al. 2027 (in prep.)), Praesepe, Coma Berenices, Alpha Persei, Hyades, and the Ursa Major Moving Group.

The ROC Catalog

The Red giants in Open Clusters (ROC) catalog is a census of red giants in nearby open clusters, aiming to establish a new generation of benchmark clusters with absolute ages determined to be better than 10%. We begin by constructing volume-limited catalogs of red giants in open clusters using the recently assembled Montreal Open Clusters and Associations (MOCA) database, which identifies thousands of associations and clusters. Our initial sample is restricted to 350 pc, referred to as ROC350, encompassing many well-studied systems while remaining largely accessible to interferometric angular diameter measurements. The figure below showcases an aitoff projection of red giants in open clusters out to 350 pc (red points; preliminary) and 1000 pc (blue points; preliminary). Additionally, two prominent and well-stuided open clusters have also been marked: Ruprecht 147 (the oldest open cluster out to 350 pc) and M67 (the oldest open cluster out to 1000 pc).

In addition to well-defined open clusters, ROC350 includes several widely dispersed, less understood stellar populations. The largest of these is the Ursa Majoris Corona, which contains 61 RGs; determining precise ages and compositions for these stars will help elucidate the origin of this enigmatic structure and may provide a unique opportunity to study the long-term dissolution of stellar clusters into the Galactic field.

We have also begun extending the sample to 1,000 pc, referred to as ROC1000, a volume within which many RGs remain sufficiently bright for asteroseismic characterization with TESS. Constructing this larger sample is more challenging, as the source catalogs become increasingly incomplete, measurement uncertainties grow, and extinction from molecular clouds (e.g., Orion and Circinus) introduces contamination along some lines of sight as seen in the figure above.
